# Maxime（@MaxAIoutreach）：我把 4 个数字产品做到 $1K MRR 以上——真正管用的 9 条经验

> Maxime 过去几年做了 4 个跨过 $1K MRR 的数字产品，其中一些继续过了 $5K MRR、一些到了 $10K+，他明确表示故意不给精确数字，重点是同一套流程在不同产品、受众和获客渠道上被重复验证。三个是 SaaS：RepliQ 帮企业批量生成个性化 outreach 视频、ScaliQ 用 AI agent 跑 LinkedIn outreach 并管理对话、NotiQ 帮 agency 和服务商找本地商家并转成会议，第四个是一个面向数字产品建设者的 Skool 社群。他的九条经验是：从足够痛且已有人付费的清晰问题出发（评估清单 5 问）；分发比创始人愿意承认的更重要，直接触达、LinkedIn 内容、YouTube creators、SEO、社群、partnerships、launch 平台、邮件营销、联盟这 9 个渠道里跑通一个可重复的就够，一个可靠渠道胜过十个半成品实验；在产品觉得没做完之前就开始卖，第一版目标是学习不是完美；早期客户要靠不能规模化的手工服务，这不是低效而是研究；围绕 outcome 而不是 feature 定位，最好的产品信息回答 3 个问题；launch 只是加速器，要接得上重定向、跟进、教育内容、testimonial、把用户变联盟等后续动作；不需要大粉丝量，他的首批客户来自 direct outreach、partnerships、communities、SEO 和 YouTube creators；激活与留存和获客同等重要，靠不断替换退订客户的增长无法规模化；速度有用但持续性更有用，多数产品不是死于某一周慢，而是死于几个月后创始人停下。

Over the past few years, I’ve built four digital products past $1K in monthly recurring revenue.

Some went on to pass $5K MRR, while others reached $10K+.

I’m intentionally keeping the exact numbers vague because the point of this article is not to show off revenue screenshots.

The important part is that I’ve repeated the process across several different products, audiences and acquisition channels.

Three are SaaS products:

1. [RepliQ](https://www.repliq.co/)
2. [ScaliQ](https://scaliq.ai/)
3. [NotiQ](https://notiq.io/)

The fourth is a [skool community](https://www.skool.com/outreach-ai-automation-agents) for people building and growing digital products.

None of them became successful overnight.

Some ideas worked quickly. Others required several changes before people started paying.

I made bad product decisions, spent time on features nobody cared about, tested acquisition channels that produced almost nothing, and launched products before they were truly ready.

But after taking four different products beyond the first $1K MRR, and some considerably further, I started noticing the same patterns.

The first $1K MRR is an important milestone because it proves that people are willing to pay for your product consistently.

But reaching it is rarely about having the most advanced product.

It is mostly about solving a clear problem, finding a repeatable way to reach the right people, and improving the product fast enough once customers start using it.

Here are the most important lessons I learned.

## 1. Start with a painful problem, not an interesting idea

A lot of founders begin with something they want to build.

That is understandable. Building is exciting.

But customers do not pay because an idea is technically interesting.

They pay because the product helps them achieve an outcome they already want.

Each product that worked for us was connected to a clear business problem.

RepliQ helps businesses create personalised outreach videos at scale.

ScaliQ helps people run LinkedIn outreach and manage conversations with an AI agent.

NotiQ helps agencies and service providers find local businesses and turn them into meetings.

The products are different, but the pattern is the same.

They help users get more customers, save time, or automate work that would otherwise need to be done manually.

When evaluating an idea, I now ask:

1. Is the problem painful enough?
2. Are people already spending money to solve it?
3. Can the result be explained in one sentence?
4. Can users see value quickly?
5. Can I reach the people experiencing this problem?

A clear problem makes everything easier: the product, the landing page, the pricing and the marketing.

## 2. Distribution matters more than founders want to admit

Founders naturally spend most of their time improving the product.

They redesign the dashboard, add features, rewrite onboarding and fix small details.

All of that can matter.

But a good product with no distribution usually goes nowhere.

For every product we built, the biggest challenge was not creating the software.

It was consistently putting it in front of people who needed it.

Different channels worked at different stages:

1. Direct outreach
2. LinkedIn content
3. YouTube creators
4. SEO
5. Communities
6. Partnerships
7. Launch platforms
8. Email marketing
9. Affiliates

You do not need to use every channel.

In fact, trying to use all of them at the same time usually creates mediocre results.

The goal is to find one channel that can repeatedly bring users.

Once one channel works, you can improve it and gradually add others.

One reliable acquisition channel is more valuable than ten unfinished experiments.

## 3. Sell before the product feels finished

Your product will never feel completely ready.

There will always be another feature to add, another bug to fix or another part of the interface to improve.

Waiting until everything is perfect is one of the easiest ways to waste six months.

The earliest version only needs to solve one valuable problem well enough for someone to pay.

Early customers are not just revenue.

They show you:

1. What users actually want
2. Where they get confused
3. Which features matter
4. What they are willing to pay for
5. Why they cancel
6. What language they use to describe the problem

This feedback is far more useful than guessing internally.

Many of our best product improvements came from support conversations, customer objections and watching how people really used the platform.

The goal of the first version is not perfection.

The goal is learning.

## 4. The first customers require work that does not scale

When starting a new product, I do things manually that I would never want to do forever.

I personally answer questions.

I help users with setup.

I review campaigns.

I look at failed results.

I speak with customers who cancel.

I manually reach out to potential partners.

This is not inefficient at the beginning.

It is research.

You need direct contact with users to understand why they buy, why they hesitate and why they leave.

Later, you can automate onboarding, improve documentation, hire support and build systems.

But automating too early can separate you from the information you need most.

Before scaling, learn why the product works.

## 5. Build around the outcome, not the feature

Users rarely care about the feature as much as the founder does.

They care about what happens after using it.

They do not necessarily want an AI agent.

They want more qualified conversations.

They do not want a lead database.

They want meetings with potential customers.

They do not want personalised video generation.

They want more replies from their outreach.

This changed how I think about product positioning.

Instead of explaining everything the software does, focus on the final result.

The best product message usually answers three questions:

1. Who is this for?
2. What result does it help them achieve?
3. Why is it different from the alternatives?

When that is clear, people understand the value faster.

## 6. Launches help, but they do not replace consistent acquisition

A launch can create attention, traffic and sales.

It can also give you social proof, testimonials and early feedback.

But a launch is not a complete growth strategy.

Many founders prepare for weeks, publish on launch day, get a spike in traffic and then disappear.

The more important question is:

What happens the following week?

A strong launch should feed into a longer system:

1. Retargeting interested visitors
2. Following up with leads
3. Publishing educational content
4. Reaching out to creators
5. Collecting testimonials
6. Improving onboarding
7. Turning users into affiliates
8. Reusing launch content across other channels

A launch is an accelerator.

It works best when the product already has a clear audience and an acquisition process behind it.

## 7. You do not need a large audience

We built these products without a large personal brand.

We had almost no followers when we started, and our audience is still relatively small today.

Our first customers did not come from having thousands of people waiting for us to launch.

They came from direct outreach, partnerships, communities, SEO, YouTube creators and speaking directly with people experiencing the problem.

This taught me that an audience can help, but it is not a requirement.

What you really need is:

1. A clear problem
2. A product that delivers a valuable result
3. A reliable way to reach the right people
4. The willingness to speak directly with potential customers

A small number of relevant customers is more valuable than a large audience that has no reason to buy from you.

You do not need to become an influencer before building a profitable product.

You need to find the people experiencing the problem and give them a compelling reason to try your solution.

## 8. Retention is where the real business begins

Getting someone to pay once is not enough.

The product only becomes stable when customers continue using it.

That means activation and retention matter as much as acquisition.

Ask:

1. Does the user reach value quickly?
2. Is onboarding simple?
3. Do they understand what to do next?
4. Are the results consistent?
5. Does the product become part of their workflow?
6. Are expectations clear before they subscribe?

A product can appear to grow while constantly replacing customers who cancel.

That is exhausting and difficult to scale.

The goal is not just to acquire more users.

It is to make the existing users successful enough to stay.

## 9. Speed matters, but consistency matters more

Building quickly is useful.

Launching quickly is useful.

Testing quickly is useful.

But most products do not fail because the founder moved too slowly for one week.

They fail because the founder stopped after a few months.

Growth usually comes from repeating the same important actions:

1. Improving the product
2. Speaking with users
3. Publishing content
4. Reaching out to prospects
5. Testing offers
6. Following up
7. Building partnerships
8. Studying retention

None of these actions feels revolutionary.

But consistently doing them creates momentum.

## What I would do starting again today

I would keep the process simple.

First, choose a painful and specific problem.

Then speak with potential customers before building too much.

Create the smallest version capable of producing the desired result.

Get the first customers manually.

Watch closely how they use it.

Improve the product around their biggest obstacles.

Choose one acquisition channel and work on it until it becomes repeatable.

Do not add more channels just because progress feels slow.

And do not confuse adding features with building a business.

## The biggest lesson

Building four products past $1K MRR, and taking some of them beyond $5K and $10K MRR, did not teach me that I had found one perfect formula.

Every product, audience and market is different.

But the fundamentals repeat.

Solve a painful problem.

Explain the outcome clearly.

Reach the right people consistently.

Launch before everything feels perfect.

Stay close to users.

Improve retention.

Repeat what works.

The first $1K MRR is not the finish line.

Some products will remain there for a while. Others can grow considerably beyond it.

But crossing that first milestone proves something important:

You have built something people are willing to pay for every month.

And once you have done it across several products, building recurring revenue becomes far less mysterious.
