# Bibhash Roy（@bibhashroykol）：两年 $0，然后每月 $125K——附三步 Stay-or-Switch 书面测试

> 作者把两位独立创始人对照摆在一起：Jason Zigelbaum 只做一款调研产品 Zigpoll，没有联合创始人、没有投资人、没有销售团队，头两年几乎什么都没发生，但他守着，之后营收每年翻一番、今年 6 月单月做到约 $125,000（他明确说明标题里的 $125K/month 指的是 MRR），全年有望约 $1.5 million，仍是单人；Alex Belogubov 五年发了 37 个产品，公开可查的 26 个项目里有 18 个收入恰好为 $0。作者先用研究解释为什么换点子总是感觉很对：Wolfram Schultz 在剑桥的多巴胺工作显示奖赏信号在「期待」时最强、真正到手后回落，Kivetz、Urminsky 与 Zheng 的目标梯度研究发现人感到接近目标就更卖力。再看数据说明一款产品赚钱有多慢：MicroConf 对 469 位独立 SaaS 创始人的调查里，到一个产品做到 $1,000/month 的中位时间是 12–18 个月；RevenueCat 追踪的 29,000+ 个订阅 App 上线满 12 个月的中位月收入不到 $50，最终只有约五分之一到过 $1,000/month；Ahrefs 发现 Google 第一名页面的平均年龄约 5 年、前十页面里只有 13.7% 不满一年。4 个月换 6 个想法的算例是总共约 120 天、每个点子约 20 天，而中位数需要 450 天，等于每个点子只拿到约 4% 的射程；更残酷的是对 1,000+ 小微 SaaS 的分析显示最常见的弃坑时点正好在约第 18 个月。Jon Yongfook 的公开曲线是最好旁证：连续发布 7 个 startup 近一年零收入后选定 Bannerbear 并守住，Aug 2019 $0/month、Apr 2020 $488/month、Oct 2020 $6,109/month、Jan 2021 $10,455/month。最后给出一小时内可做完的三步书面测试：Step 1 依 Annie Duke 的规则提前写下 kill criteria（一句同时含日期、数字、未达标动作的话，例如到 2027 年 3 月 1 日没有 10 个付费客户或 $500 MRR 就砍掉）；Step 2 每月同一天书面回答 Gail Goodman 的两个问题——有没有客户在我没推销的情况下自己表达了需要、数字是否比上月好一点；Step 3 按 Rob Walling 的 stair-step 方法给「想造新东西」的冲动一个小出口，新点子只写进 ideas.txt 而不是新开 repo。

I compared him with a founder who launched 37 products — most made $0. The difference wasn’t the ideas. It was staying long enough.

Two solo founders. Same skill level. Same amount of time. Completely different bank accounts.

Founder one: [Jason Zigelbaum](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/tech/hitting-125k-mrr-as-a-solo-founder-by-doubling-down-on-the-right-segment-c4o2Tfs6mjdpip5yZhaO) built one survey product, Zigpoll. No co-founder, no investors, no sales team. For about two years, almost nothing happened. He stayed on it anyway. Revenue then doubled every year — and this June he closed the month at roughly $125,000 in monthly revenue, on track for about $1.5 million a year. Still solo.

Founder two: [Alex Belogubov](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/ive-launched-37-products-in-5-years-and-not-doing-that-again-0b66e6e8b3) launched 37 products in 5 years. His own words: one went viral, and “almost all the others struggle to get any traction at all.” In [a public tally of 26 of those projects](https://www.buildmvpfast.com/blog/indie-hacking-real-numbers-4-years-26-projects-115k-2026), 18 made exactly $0.

Same game. Opposite strategies. And the strategy that felt more productive — always be launching — is the one that kept producing zeros.

If you’re a technical founder juggling your third or fourth idea this year, this article is about why that keeps happening, what the revenue data actually says about how long one product takes to pay, and a simple written test that tells you when switching is smart and when it’s just the itch talking.

> Quick definitions (used throughout): SaaS = software sold as a monthly subscription. MRR = monthly recurring revenue — the subscription money that repeats every month. “$125K/month” in the title is MRR.

## 🔁 Why a new idea always feels like the right move

Here’s the uncomfortable part: switching ideas doesn’t feel like quitting. It feels like progress. There are two well-studied reasons for that.

👉 Your brain rewards anticipation more than results. Neuroscience research on dopamine — most famously[ Wolfram Schultz’s work at Cambridg](https://mindlabneuroscience.com/neuroscience-of-novelty/)e — shows the reward chemical spikes hardest when you’re expecting something good, and drops once you actually have it. A brand-new product idea is pure expectation: no churned users yet, no quiet launch, no flat chart. Day one of idea number six literally feels better than day 200 of idea number one — chemically, before any facts arrive.

👉 Starting fresh manufactures fake progress. A[ well-known study by Kivetz, Urminsky and Zhen](https://home.uchicago.edu/ourminsky/Goal-Gradient_Illusionary_Goal_Progress.pdf)g found people work harder when they feel close to a goal — even when that feeling of progress is artificial. A new idea gives you exactly that: within a week you have a name, a repo, a landing page. It looks like you covered more ground in 7 days than the old idea covered in 3 months. You didn’t. You just moved the starting line and ran the easy first stretch again.

One founder in [a thread of $0 post-mortems](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/how-i-failed-5-side-projects-in-6-years-earning-0-5e7bb3bd55) put it plainly: “the thrill is in getting something up and running, then it quickly fades and I drop to something else.”

So the switching isn’t a discipline problem. It’s your reward system doing exactly what it’s built to do. Which is why willpower doesn’t fix it — a written rule does. We’ll get there.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

## 📉 The math: one product’s revenue is slower than you think

Now put feelings aside and look at what the data says about how long ONE product takes to make real money:

- 💰 12–18 months to reach $1,000/month — that’s the median time per product, from[ MicroConf’s survey of 469 independent SaaS founder](https://microconf.com/state-of-indie-saas)s. Half of successful products take even longer.
- 📉 Under $50/month at the one-year mark — the median monthly revenue for a subscription app 12 months after launch, across[ 29,000+ apps tracked by RevenueCa](https://techcrunch.com/2024/03/12/most-subscription-mobile-apps-dont-make-money-new-report-shows/)t. Only about 1 in 5 ever reaches $1,000/month at all.
- 🔎 Google traffic takes years, not months —[ Ahrefs’ ranking stud](https://ahrefs.com/blog/how-long-does-it-take-to-rank-in-google-and-how-old-are-top-ranking-pages/)y found the average page holding the #1 spot in Google is about 5 years old, and only 13.7% of top-10 pages are less than a year old. Google mostly sends visitors to pages that have been around for a long time. So when you abandon an idea, every blog post you wrote for it gets thrown away before it was ever old enough for Google to start sending it visitors. All that writing work earned nothing — not because it was bad, but because you didn’t wait.

Now run the switching math. Scope: one founder, working evenings and weekends, per product idea.

> 📐 Worked example: Say you cycle through 6 ideas in 4 months — about 120 days total, so each idea gets roughly 20 days of your attention before you move on. The median product needs 12–18 months — call it 450 days — of continuous work to hit its first $1,000/month. So each of your 6 ideas received about 20 ÷ 450 ≈ 4% of the runway the median winner needed. You didn’t test 6 ideas. You ran 4% of one test, six times.

That’s the whole trap in one calculation. The assets that eventually produce revenue — Google rankings, word-of-mouth, an email list, your own understanding of the customer — all compound slowly and privately. None of them show up on a dashboard in the first months. Every switch throws them away at their least visible and most valuable stage.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

## 🧨 Month 18: the worst possible moment to quit — and the most popular

Here’s the cruelest detail in the data. An [analysis of 1,000+ small SaaS products](https://www.rockingweb.com.au/micro-saas-revenue-analysis-2025/) found the most common quitting point is around month 18 — which is exactly the point where the 12–18 month median says first meaningful revenue arrives. Founders give up, on average, at the doorstep.

You can watch it happen in public. [Jon Yongfook](https://www.bannerbear.com/journey-to-10k-mrr/) spent almost a year launching 7 startups with zero revenue — none even had a way to charge money. Then he picked ONE (Bannerbear, an image-generation tool) and stayed. The chart of what happened next is the whole argument:

- ❌ Aug 2019: $0/month — after a year of serial launching
- 😐 Dec 2019: $400/month — four months of focus, barely anything
- 😐 Apr 2020: $488/month — eight months in, almost flat. This is where switchers switch.
- ✅ Oct 2020: $6,109/month — the compounding finally surfaces
- ✅ Jan 2021: $10,455/month — 16 months on one product

His verdict afterwards: “Never half-ass two things. Whole-ass one thing.” Look at that April 2020 number again — $488 after 8 months. By every gut-feel standard, that’s a failing product. It was actually 6 months from $6K. A long flat stretch does not mean your product failed. It is the normal waiting period almost every product goes through before the money shows up — and you cannot skip it by starting over.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

## 🤔 “But Pieter Levels launches something new every month”

Fair objection. The most famous indie founder alive [publicly advises](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=16482674): don’t spend more than a month or two on an idea that isn’t getting traction. And he’s rich. So which is it — stay or switch?

Look closer at what he actually does. His 12-startups-in-12-months run produced mostly dead projects — his own description is that most “just disappeared.” But the moment two of them (Nomad List and Remote OK) showed a real pull, he stopped launching and spent years compounding those two. His bets were cheap, tiny, and honest tests — and the instant one caught, he became the most committed non-switcher in the game.

The difference between his pattern and the 6-ideas-in-4-months pattern:

- ✅ Cheap test, honest verdict, then all-in on the winner — that’s a portfolio.
- ❌ Half-built product, no verdict, fresh start whenever the plateau gets boring — that’s a graveyard.

The problem was never switching itself. It’s switching without a written verdict — which means the decision is being made by the dopamine, not by you.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

## 🧭 The Stay-or-Switch Test — decide on paper, not on mood

Here’s the save-worthy part: a three-step written test that separates a legitimate switch from an itch-driven one. Do this tonight — it takes under an hour.

👉 Step 1 — Write down, today, exactly when you would stop.

A “kill criteria” is one written sentence that says exactly when you will stop working on this product. Decision scientist Annie Duke’s [rule for founders](https://review.firstround.com/grit-or-quit-tactical-advice-for-founders-facing-tough-company-building-decisions/) is simple: decide when you would quit while you are calm — because in the middle of a bad week, your judgement is the worst it will ever be.

- 🖊️ Open a new note. Title it: “[Your product] — kill criteria”
- 🖊️ Write ONE sentence that contains three things: a date, a number, and what you will do if you miss it
- 🔒 Until that date arrives: switching is off the table. No exceptions, no bad-week re-votes
- 🔓 After that date: if you missed the number, killing it is honest — and staying is what needs a reason

> 📐 Worked example: “If by March 1, 2027 (12 months in) I don’t have 10 paying customers or $500 MRR, I kill this product and write one page on why.” Scope: one product, one deadline, checked once on that date — not re-argued every bad week.

👉 Step 2 — Once a month, answer the same two questions in writing.

Gail Goodman took Constant Contact through [a famously slow early grind to a billion-dollar exit](https://businessofsoftware.org/talks/gail-goodman-constant-contact-how-to-negotiate-the-long-slow-saas-ramp-of-death/). Her test for whether a slow product is worth staying on has only two questions. Pick one day a month, and write one dated line for each:

- ❓ Question 1: Did any customer show they want this — without me pushing them? Examples that count: someone asked when a feature is coming, someone renewed, someone told a friend about it
- ❓ Question 2: Are my numbers better than last month — even a little? They don’t need to be big. They need to be moving up
- ✅ Both answers yes → you stay. However slow it feels, the product is working — it’s just early
- ❌ Either answer no, month after month → now a switch is a legitimate business decision, not the itch talking

👉 Step 3 — Give the build-something-new urge a smaller, safer outlet.

The urge to start something fresh will not disappear. Fighting it with willpower fails. Instead, give it a small place to live that doesn’t cost you your main product:

- 🛠️ Want to build something new? Build something small that FEEDS the main product — a free template, a small plugin, a one-time-purchase tool that brings people to it. This is[ Rob Walling’s stair-step approac](https://robwalling.com/essays/2015/03/26/the-stair-step-method-of-bootstrapping)h: small builds that stack, instead of rival products that compete for your time
- 📝 Got a shiny new product idea mid-plateau? It goes into a text file called ideas.txt — not into a new code repository
- ⏳ The ideas wait until your kill-criteria date. If the idea is still exciting on verdict day, it earned a real look. Most won’t be

(Zigelbaum’s version of Step 2, by the way: he watched which customers expanded without being asked and rebuilt the product around them — his one regret is not acting on that signal a year earlier.)

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

## ✍️ The boring truth

Staying on one product through a flat chart is genuinely boring. Nobody posts screenshots of month 11 at $488. But the founders getting paid are, overwhelmingly, the ones who were willing to be boring for 12–18 months while the invisible assets stacked up — and the founders at $0 are, overwhelmingly, the ones who kept treating the most valuable stretch of the curve as a signal to leave.

The itch will tell you the next idea is different. The next idea is not different. The next idea is day one again.

— Bibhash
