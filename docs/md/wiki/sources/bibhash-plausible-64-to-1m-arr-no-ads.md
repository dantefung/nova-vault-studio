# Bibhash Roy（@bibhashroykol）：Plausible 从每月 $64 到每年 $1M，零广告、零联盟分成——五个免费动作与执行顺序

> 作者按 Plausible 自己的公开复盘逐篇读下来：2019 年 5 月 14 日第一个付费订阅、当月月底 MRR 只有 $64；2020 年 4 月 2 日（第一个客户之后 324 天）才到 $415 MRR；2021 年 10 月 $500,000 ARR、4,802 个付费客户；2022 年 6 月 2 日突破 $1 million ARR（$83,637 MRR）、团队四人；按其官网，截至 2026 年 10 月是 21,000 个付费订阅、10 人团队，而这三年里没投过一分广告、没付过一笔联盟分成。他特意纠正网上反复流传的「$3.1M a year」并非出自 Plausible。动作顺序是：第一，把首页标题从「Simple analytics for your website」改成「Simple and privacy-friendly alternative to Google Analytics」，直接点名巨头并复用买家已在 Google 里输入的词；第二，2020 年 4 月 8 日 Marko Šarić 发出约 2,500 词的《Why you should stop using Google Analytics on your website》，文中连竞品一起列出、把 Plausible 放到接近结尾才提，他自己在 Hacker News 发帖，第一周带来 48,000+ 独立访客（此前约 15 个月全站总共才 27,300 访客）、166 个免费试用注册；第三，2020 年 4 月 1 日至 7 月 30 日按来源统计，Hacker News 给 43,600 访客、Google 搜索只给 6,300 访客却成了试用注册第一大来源，4、5 月开试用的人有 33.5% 最终付费；第四，给写「best Google Analytics alternatives」的人发信，多数无视，但 Opensource.com 的一次回复换来单日 94 个试用注册。作者也诚实标出抄不来的运气：2022 年 1、2 月奥地利和法国监管机构裁定网站使用 Google Analytics 违反 GDPR，MRR 从 2021 年 10 月的 $42,624 涨到 2022 年 1 月的 $55,411；2022 年 3 月 Google 宣布关停旧版 Google Analytics，那个月新增 $8,247 MRR。最后他把这套顺序命名为 Plausible Order，并用虚构创始人 Meera 演示五步，含至少 15 个买家搜索词、1,500 到 2,500 词的文章、四到六个分发阵地、连续 14 天每天回复两个人，以及 Reddit 2,000 访客 12 试用（0.6%）对 Google 300 访客 9 试用（3%）的算例。

One blog post brought 48,000 visitors in a week, and Google search turned them into paying customers. What a bootstrapped founder can copy this week, and the lucky breaks Plausible admits you can’t.

👋 In May 2019, a small analytics tool called Plausible was making $64 a month. On June 2, 2022, it crossed $1 million a year.

In those three years it did not pay for a single ad. It did not pay a single affiliate. Its founders [say so in their own write-up](https://plausible.io/blog/bootstrapping-saas), and the milestone figures in this article all come from their own posts.

I went through those posts in order, from the first paying customer onward. What they show is not one clever trick. It is a short list of free, unexciting moves, done in a specific order, by people with no marketing budget. That order is what a solo founder can copy.

> 📖 Three terms used throughout:

> SaaS = software you sell as a subscription, paid every month or every year.

> MRR = monthly recurring revenue, the money your subscribers pay you every month. ARR = annual recurring revenue, your MRR times 12. So $83,637 MRR is about $1 million ARR.

> Affiliate program = a deal where you pay outsiders a share of every sale they send you, for example 25% of each payment.

## 📈 The Climb, in Plausible’s Own Numbers

- 🌱 May 14, 2019: first paying subscriber. MRR at the end of that month:[ $6](https://plausible.io/blog/open-source-saas)4.
- 🐢 April 2, 2020: $415 MRR,[ 324 days after the first custome](https://plausible.io/blog/startup-marketing)r. Almost a full year to reach $400 a month.
- 💰 October 2021: $500,000 ARR, with[ 4,802 paying customer](https://plausible.io/blog/bootstrapping-saas)s.
- 🏆 June 2, 2022: $1 million ARR ($83,637 MRR), with[ a team of fou](https://plausible.io/blog/open-source-saas)r.
- 📊 Today (October 2026): 21,000 paying subscribers and a team of 10, according to[ Plausible’s own homepag](https://plausible.io/)e.

❌ One number you will see repeated online is not from Plausible: “$3.1M a year”. That is an estimate by a revenue-tracking website. Plausible stopped publishing revenue after 2022. Its [2026 post](https://plausible.io/blog/ignore-startup-advice) only says that revenue has grown several times since it passed $1 million.

## 🐢 Year One: A Good Product, Almost No Buyers

The first year looks like most solo founders’ first year. Plausible’s builder, Uku Täht, posted his progress on Indie Hackers (a website where founders share what they are building) and made the code public in September 2019, so anyone could read it.

The product worked. People who found it liked it. But after 324 days it earned $415 a month. Building in public brought the first users, but after almost a full year, revenue was still only about $400 a month.

The turn came in March 2020, when Marko Šarić joined as the second founder to handle marketing. On his first day, March 16, 2020, the website had [49 visitors, and zero of them came from Google](https://plausible.io/blog/open-source-saas).

🏷️ Move 1: They Named the Giant in the Headline

The first change was one line on the homepage. The headline went from “Simple analytics for your website” to “Simple and privacy-friendly alternative to Google Analytics”, according to [Plausible’s own account](https://plausible.io/blog/startup-marketing).

The old headline described the product. The new one told a stranger, in one line, which product Plausible replaces and why someone would switch. It also used the exact words buyers were already typing into Google: “Google Analytics alternative”.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

## ✍️ Move 2: One Blog Post That Brought 48,000 Visitors in a Week

On April 8, 2020, Marko published “Why you should stop using Google Analytics on your website”. It was about 2,500 words of plain reasons, it listed other alternatives as well (including competitors), and it named Plausible only near the end.

He posted it to Hacker News himself. Hacker News is a news website run by the startup investor Y Combinator, where readers (mostly software developers) vote links up or down.

What that one post did, in its first week, according to [Plausible’s write-up](https://plausible.io/blog/blog-post-changed-my-startup):

- 👀 48,000+ unique visitors. The site had seen 27,300 visitors in the whole of the previous 15 months or so.
- 🧪 166 free-trial signups. That was more than in the previous four months combined.
- 🔍 People searching Google for “Plausible” by name went from about 10 searches a week to 121.

> 💡 Why the post worked: a reader who never bought Plausible still got something useful from it: an honest list of reasons, plus other tools to try. Marko then shared it himself in six places where his buyers already read: Hacker News, Indie Hackers, Lobsters (a smaller Hacker News-style site), Reddit’s r/degoogle forum, Twitter and Mastodon.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

## 🔍 Move 3: Google Search Kept Paying After Hacker News Faded

The crowd from Hacker News gave Plausible its biggest week. It did not give the most customers. Plausible published its traffic by source for [April 1 to July 30, 2020](https://plausible.io/blog/startup-marketing):

- 📰 Hacker News: 43,600 visitors.
- 🔎 Google search: 6,300 visitors. Yet Google was the number one source of trial signups.

So Google sent about one-seventh of the visitors Hacker News sent, and still produced more trials than any other source. Someone who searches “Google Analytics alternative” is already shopping. Someone who clicks a popular Hacker News link is mostly curious.

Plausible kept writing about one post a week, picking topics from the suggestions Google itself shows under a search. Clicks from Google rose from about 400 in the months before to over 6,000 in those four months. And 33.5% of the people who started a trial in April and May 2020 went on to pay.

- ❌ A wave from a news site fades within days.
- ✅ A page that ranks for a phrase buyers search keeps sending buyers every day, without more work.

📣 Move 4: They Went Where People Were Already Complaining

Two more free moves from the same months, both [from Plausible’s own account](https://plausible.io/blog/startup-marketing):

- 📧 Emails to writers of “best Google Analytics alternatives” posts. “Most sites ended up ignoring our messages,” Plausible wrote. But one yes, from Opensource.com, brought[ 94 trial signups in a single da](https://plausible.io/blog/open-source-saas)y.
- 🐦 Replies on Twitter (now X). Marko regularly searched Twitter for people complaining about Google Analytics, and answered them. With only 674 followers, Twitter became Plausible’s second-biggest source of visitors in those four months.

## ⚠️ The Honest Catch: The Lucky Breaks Plausible Admits

The fastest growth came in 2022, alongside two outside events that Plausible itself credits.

- ⚖️ January and February 2022: privacy regulators in Austria and France [ruled that websites using Google Analytics broke GDPR](https://www.goodwinprivacyblog.com/2022/02/18/use-of-google-analytics-by-eu-websites-violates-gdpr/), the European Union’s privacy law. Plausible’s MRR went from $42,624 in October 2021 to $55,411 in January 2022.
- 🪦 March 2022: Google announced it would shut down its older version of Google Analytics. That month, Plausible added[ $8,247 of new MR](https://plausible.io/blog/open-source-saas)R, its best month up to then.

Plausible’s own write-up says that sometimes a startup needs to be lucky. Its second founder was also an experienced content writer, not a developer learning to write. So copy the order of the moves. Do not count on a regulator, or on one post reaching 48,000 people.

💸 What Saying No to Affiliates Cost

The only cost Plausible has put in writing is one paragraph from 2020. Some bloggers left Plausible out of their “best tools” posts [“after we tell them we don’t have an affiliate program”](https://plausible.io/blog/best-marketing-practices), and listed tools that paid them instead. Plausible has never published a number for what that cost.

Marko was asked about it again in 2025. His answer on affiliates: [“we haven’t tried it, so we don’t know.”](https://saasology.castos.com/episodes/how-plausible-grew-to-1m-arr-without-ads-or-investors-marko-saric-co-founder-of-plausible) Meanwhile Plausible’s two closest rivals both pay for referrals: Fathom pays affiliates [25% for the life of each customer](https://usefathom.com/blog/affiliate-program), and Simple Analytics runs a [customer referral program](https://docs.simpleanalytics.com/affiliate/terms).

> 🧭 The fair reading: refusing affiliates did not stop Plausible from reaching $1 million a year. That does not prove refusing them helped. Plausible said no for its own reasons (it did not want to feed the ad-tracking business it competes against, and it did not have time to check that affiliates disclosed their links and stayed away from paid ads), not because it measured affiliates and found them weak. If you want the numbers on when an affiliate program can pay for an indie SaaS, I worked through them[ in my October 20 articl](https://rapidflowautomation.beehiiv.com/p/he-paid-about-100-a-month-for-an-affiliate-program-got-16-affiliates-and-3-clicks-and-called-it-a-co)e.

## 🧩 The Plausible Order: 5 Free Moves You Can Run This Month

I call this the Plausible Order: the moves above, in the order Plausible made them, written so one person with no budget can do them alone. The order matters. The headline comes first because the blog post points readers to it. The measuring comes last because you need traffic before there is anything to count.

To show each step, I’ll use a made-up founder, Meera. She sells a simple invoicing tool for freelance designers. Her giant, the product her buyers use today instead of hers, is QuickBooks.

🏷️ Step 1: Put the Giant’s Name in Your Headline

What to do: rewrite your homepage headline so a stranger sees, in one line, which product you replace and for whom.

How:

- 👉 1️⃣ Write down the one product your buyer would use if yours did not exist. One name, not a category (“QuickBooks”, not “accounting software”).
- 👉 2️⃣ Write the headline in this shape: “[the one thing you do better] alternative to [giant], for [who]”. If you have not chosen that one thing yet, my[ Calendly articl](https://rapidflowautomation.beehiiv.com/p/a-10-price-cut-costs-a-saas-50-of-its-profit-the-saas-positioning-playbook-that-beat-a-3b-calendly-a)e walks through how to pick it.
- 👉 3️⃣ Publish one page titled “[Your product] vs [giant]” with five plain differences, one short paragraph each, and link it from your site’s footer.

✅ Done when: the new headline is live and the comparison page is linked from your footer.

🧪 Meera’s version: “Simple invoicing alternative to QuickBooks, for freelance designers.” Her comparison page is “Meera’s tool vs QuickBooks”.

🔎 Step 2: Collect the Exact Words Buyers Type

What to do: make a list of the phrases people already type into Google when they want to leave your giant.

How:

- 👉 1️⃣ Open a private browser window (so your own search history does not change the results).
- 👉 2️⃣ Type “[giant] alternative” into Google, and before pressing Enter, write down every suggestion in the dropdown.
- 👉 3️⃣ Press Enter. Copy every question in the “People also ask” box and every phrase under “Related searches” at the bottom of the page.
- 👉 4️⃣ Repeat steps 2 and 3 with “[giant] vs”, “stop using [giant]” and “[giant] too [the problem you fix]”, for example “QuickBooks too complicated”.
- 👉 5️⃣ Paste every phrase into one column of a spreadsheet. In the next column, mark yes or no: does my product actually fix this?

✅ Done when: you have at least 15 phrases, with a yes or no next to each.

✍️ Step 3: Write One “Why You Should Stop Using [Giant]” Post

What to do: write one long, honest post built from your “yes” phrases, useful even to readers who never buy from you.

How:

- 👉 1️⃣ Make the first line a plain disclosure: “I make [product], an alternative to [giant]. Here is the honest case for switching, and for staying.” Hacker News commenters criticised Plausible’s post for not saying this clearly up front.
- 👉 2️⃣ Write one section for each of your five to seven strongest “yes” phrases. Back each one with a link: the giant’s own pricing page, its help pages, or public forum threads where users describe the problem.
- 👉 3️⃣ Add a section listing four to six alternatives, competitors included, with one honest line about who each one suits.
- 👉 4️⃣ Mention your own product last, in one short paragraph.
- 👉 5️⃣ Aim for 1,500 to 2,500 words, and put your main phrase in the page address, for example /stop-using-quickbooks-for-freelance-invoicing.

✅ Done when: the post is live, and a friend who has never used your product can tell you one thing they learned from it.

📣 Step 4: Put the Post in Front of Buyers Yourself, the Same Day

What to do: share the post where your buyers already gather, and ask the people who write “best tools” lists to include you.

How:

- 👉 1️⃣ List four to six places where your buyers already talk. For example: a subreddit (a topic forum on Reddit), a Slack or Discord group for your audience, Indie Hackers, or Hacker News if your buyers are developers.
- 👉 2️⃣ Read each place’s rules on self-promotion first. Where it is allowed, post with a title that names the problem, not your product.
- 👉 3️⃣ Search Google for “best [giant] alternatives”. Email the writers of the top 10 results with the message below.
- 👉 4️⃣ Save a search on X or Reddit for your giant’s name plus a complaint word (“expensive”, “confusing”, “alternative”). Reply helpfully to two people a day, and link your post only when it answers their actual question.

✅ Done when: the post is in at least four places, ten emails are sent, and you have replied to two people a day for 14 days.

> ✉️ The email to list writers:

> “Hi [name], I read your post ’[post title]’. I make [product], an alternative to [giant] for [who]. If you update the list, here is a one-line description you can use: [one line]. I’m not offering payment, just the information. Happy to answer any questions. [Your name]”

> Expect most people not to reply. Plausible’s one yes was worth 94 trials in a day.

📊 Step 5: Judge It by Trials From Each Source, Not by Visitors

What to do: for 30 days, count visitors and trial signups separately for each source, and put your hours where the signup rate is highest.

How:

- 👉 1️⃣ For every trial signup, record where the person came from: read it from your analytics tool, or add a “How did you hear about us?” box to your signup form.
- 👉 2️⃣ Every Monday, write two numbers for each source in a spreadsheet: visitors that week, and trial signups that week.
- 👉 3️⃣ For each source, divide trials by visitors. That is the source’s signup rate.
- 👉 4️⃣ On day 30, open Google Search Console (Google’s free tool that shows which searches your pages appear for) and check whether your post is appearing for the phrases from Step 2.
- 👉 5️⃣ Write one decision in one sentence: write the next post on a new phrase, rewrite this one around different phrases, or put more hours into the community that converts best.

✅ Done when: you have four Mondays of numbers and one written decision.

> 🧮 Worked example (Meera, first 30 days, counted per source):

> 👉 Reddit sent 2,000 visitors and 12 trials. 12 ÷ 2,000 = 0.6%.

> 👉 Google sent 300 visitors and 9 trials. 9 ÷ 300 = 3%.

> 👉 3% ÷ 0.6% = 5. Each Google visitor was 5 times more likely to start a trial than each Reddit visitor.

> Reddit brought the crowd and Google brought the buyers, the same split as Plausible’s Hacker News and Google numbers. Meera’s decision: write her second post on the next “yes” phrase in her list.

> What each outcome means for you: if search has the higher rate, write more search posts. If a community has the higher rate, keep showing up in that community. If every source is under 0.5%, the post is not about a problem your buyers have yet, so rewrite it around different phrases from Step 2.

## 🎯 The Takeaway

Plausible’s free marketing was not one big idea. It was a headline that names the giant, one honest post built from the words buyers search, sharing that post by hand where buyers already were, and then putting the hours into whichever source produced paying customers. None of it cost money, and all of it is slow and unglamorous.

Ads and affiliates can come later, if at all. What you cannot skip is doing these free moves in this order, because each one feeds the next: the headline gives the post somewhere to send readers, and the post gives you traffic to measure.
